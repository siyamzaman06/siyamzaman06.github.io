01 · Modeling approach

Starting with the duct, then the full assembly

I chose to compare outlet velocity and pressure drop before running the full thermal model. That let me screen more ducts faster, then choose geometries for the physical experiment.

Why I started with duct-only simulations

I set up the duct, aluminum block, and heat sink, then chose to compare ducts before running the full thermal analysis. Without the fan geometry, the model could not predict how evenly airflow would leave the fan.

The first comparisons used outlet velocity and pressure drop, without the thermal components. This let me screen duct shapes faster before adding heat transfer.

Mesh refinement around the heat sink

In the full assembly model, I refined the mesh around the heat sink to resolve its thin fins.

Compare the pressure each duct needs

To compare outlets, I needed to understand how much pressure each duct required at a given airflow. I focused on duct geometry, flow rate, and pressure drop, prescribing several inlet velocities and recording the required static pressure.

The D90 and rounded 70 × 40 sweeps followed the expected ΔP = KQ² relationship; matched-flow results supplied the comparison coefficients for D115 and 70 × 70. This gave me a basis for comparing resistance before committing to fabrication.

Check what the fan can actually deliver

A smaller outlet increased velocity at the imposed flow, but also increased the pressure requirement. I intersected each duct curve with the fan P–Q curve to check what airflow the fan could actually sustain. For the rounded 70 × 40 mm outlet, that changed the predicted speed from the imposed 12.97 m/s to about 7.0 m/s.

I selected that outlet for testing because it still had the highest predicted operating velocity among the four documented cases and closely matched the heat-sink footprint. I accepted lower total airflow in exchange for a more concentrated outlet flow; whether that improves cooling remains a physical-test question.

02 · Fan operating point

Fan operating point and system resistance

Resistance determines delivered airflow

The D90 and rounded 70 × 40 mm sweeps, together with matched-flow results for the other two ducts, supplied the system curves. Their intersections with the ARCTIC P12 Pro P–Q curve give the predicted operating airflow.

Shown here4 system curves
Selected flow≈42 CFM70 × 40 case
Selected speed≈7.0 m/sPredicted outlet average
Operating point: ΔPfan(Q) = ΔPduct(Q)

The 70 × 40 mm duct has the steepest resistance curve. The fan therefore operates near 42 CFM, not the 76.9 CFM imposed in the highest-flow CFD case.

Reading the units

CFM is total airflow; m/s is outlet speed. Converting between them requires the outlet area. Pressure converts directly.

Flow → speedm/s = CFM ÷ (2,118.88 × area in m²)
Speed → flowCFM = m/s × area in m² × 2,118.88
Pa → mmH₂OmmH₂O = Pa ÷ 9.80665
mmH₂O → PaPa = mmH₂O × 9.80665
Conclusion

Selected for testing: rounded 70 × 40 mm outlet

It had the highest fan-corrected outlet velocity and matches the 67 × 36 mm heat-sink footprint. Physical testing will compare its temperature response with the 70 × 70 mm duct.

04 · Support fixture

Heater-block support fixture

The open PLA fixture supports the heater assembly and keeps the hot block off the printed frame.

Designed a four-post support fixture

Four raised posts carry the M3 fasteners while the open frame keeps the hot block off the PLA and limits print material.

Checked fixture stress under gravity loading

The static study checks the combined gravity load through the four posts. Low-stress material was removed while the load paths and interfaces stayed intact.

3D-printed and assembled the fixture

The printed mount carries the aluminum block and heat sink in the test setup. M3 fasteners keep the fixture serviceable between duct tests.

05 · Fabrication & assembly

Machining and assembly

I machined the aluminum block, mounted the power resistor, soldered its leads, and assembled the test setup.

Machining the heater block

I drilled and tapped the aluminum block for the heater assembly. One mounting hole was misplaced during machining; the block remains in use in this test setup.

Mounting and soldering the power resistor

I fastened the power resistor to the aluminum block and soldered its leads. I applied thermal paste between the resistor and block, between the heat sink and block, and at the temperature-sensor attachment points where applicable. The resistor supplies heat to the block for the duct comparisons.

Assembling the cooling rig and instrumentation

I assembled the heat sink and heater block on the printed support and connected the bench wiring and instrumentation. The setup is ready for the comparison ducts; airflow and temperature measurements are still pending.

06 · Python data logging

From serial readings to a live temperature graph

Reading the Arduino serial monitor alone was inefficient: the scrolling numbers made it difficult to follow temperature changes over time, and recording readings manually added extra work. I wrote a Python script to read the serial data, automatically save it to a CSV file that opens in Excel, and display a graph that updates live as new readings arrive.

Individual readings were hard to track

The serial monitor showed the current temperature, but following the overall trend meant watching a stream of numbers and manually transferring readings for analysis.

See the trend and save the data automatically

The Python script records time and temperature in a CSV file for later analysis in Excel while updating the temperature-versus-time graph during the run. This makes the temperature response easier to follow and removes the need to copy readings by hand.

Current issue · Fan tach signal

Check the tach wire with an oscilloscope

The fan tach signal is not giving me a reliable RPM reading yet. I plan to inspect it with an oscilloscope before deciding whether to add a 74HCT14 for signal conditioning.

I want to record RPM alongside temperature so I can check whether the fan speed changes between ducts at the same control setting.

Temperature comparisons made before RPM measurement is working will be preliminary.

Print two test ducts

Print the 70 × 70 and rounded 70 × 40 mm ducts. A removable flow straightener may be tested separately.

Keep the comparison consistent

Use the same heater block, heat sink, input power, and fan control setting for each duct. RPM measurement will check whether the actual speed stays the same.

Compare temperature response

Measure how the temperature changes with each outlet. The fastest predicted airflow is a reason to test a duct, not proof that it cools best.
